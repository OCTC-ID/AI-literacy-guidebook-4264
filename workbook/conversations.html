Two Kinds of Conversations

Most conversations about AI have nothing to do with misconduct. A student asks whether they can use a tool for a project. A checkpoint note tells you something you didn't expect. Someone mentions in class that they tried AI on a problem and it didn't help. These everyday conversations are where most of the BE behaviors actually get taught, and they go better when talking about AI already feels normal in your course.

Once in a while, you'll have a concern about a specific piece of work. That's a different conversation, and it helps to have a plan before you need one.

Everyday conversations

The goal is to make AI something students can mention without worrying about getting in trouble. A few ways to get there:

  • Bring it up yourself early in the term, when nothing is at stake. Walk through your AI use statement and ask what questions students have.
  • Ask about process as a normal part of feedback: "How did you get started on this?" or "What did you check before you turned it in?" Ask it whether or not you think AI was involved.
  • When a student tells you how they used AI, thank them first, even if the use wasn't what you had in mind. How you respond decides whether they'll tell you next time.

When you have a concern

Start with curiosity here too. Ask the student to walk you through the work: how they started, where their information came from, why they made a particular choice. Ask them to explain a paragraph or a step in their own words. The purpose is to understand what happened, and the conversation often clears things up. Sometimes a student misunderstood what was allowed. Sometimes they did the work and simply write or solve problems differently than you expected.

Keep the conversation separate from any decision. You don't have to decide anything while you're talking with the student.

Detector scores aren't evidence

AI detection tools give a guess, not a finding. They produce false positives, and research has found they're more likely to flag writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated. That matters at a college where many students are multilingual. A detector score shouldn't be the reason for a concern conversation, and it shouldn't be the basis for a sanction.

Better evidence comes from the work and the process behind it: drafts or version history, the student's own AI disclosure and checkpoint notes, how the work compares with the assignment's AI label, and what the student can explain when you talk.

Your two policies set the rules

Every instructor writes two policies for the syllabus: an AI use policy and an academic integrity policy. Together, they're the rules your students are held to in your course.

The KCTCS Code of Student Conduct describes cheating as using unauthorized help or not following the rules or instructions for the work. For AI, your AI use policy and the AI label on each assignment say what's authorized. Your academic integrity policy says what happens when something isn't. If your AI policy doesn't say what was allowed, it's hard to call a use unauthorized. If your academic integrity policy doesn't mention AI, students can't know what's at stake.

When a conversation shows that your instructions left room for confusion, the fix is usually to clarify them, not to penalize the student. Exercise 1 is where you write your AI use policy and connect it to your academic integrity policy. Exercise 2 carries it into a specific assignment.

Where your role ends

If, after looking at the evidence and talking with the student, you conclude that academic dishonesty happened, the KCTCS Code of Student Conduct sets out what comes next:

  • You choose the academic sanction, within what the Code allows and what your academic integrity policy says. Faculty can require the work to be redone, lower the grade on the assignment or in the course, give a zero, or give a failing grade for the course. Anything beyond that, such as removal from the course or suspension, is a recommendation or referral, not your decision alone.
  • You report it. Within ten business days, send a written description of what happened and the sanction you gave to your supervisor and the chief academic officer or designee.
  • You tell the student in writing. Explain the offense and the sanction, and let them know they have the right to appeal.
  • The college handles the rest. Appeals start with you and can move to an administrator, then the chancellor, then the College Appeals Board. Repeat offenses and disciplinary action are handled by the college, not by individual faculty.

A student who receives an academic penalty for dishonesty can't withdraw from the course to avoid it.

One more case: if AI is used to harass someone or create sexual content about a real person, such as fake images of a classmate, that isn't an academic integrity matter. Report it through the college's Title IX process.

Paste this into Copilot to practice a conversation. Copilot plays a student, and you play yourself. Type "stop" when you're done, and it will give you feedback. Use a made-up situation, not a real student.

I'm a community college instructor practicing conversations with
students about AI. I want you to play a student so I can practice.

Setup:
- Ask me which kind of conversation I want to practice:
  1. Everyday: a student asking whether they can use AI, or telling
     me how they used it.
  2. Concern: I have questions about how a student completed an
     assignment.
- Then ask me for the course and the assignment, and what my AI
  policy says for that assignment.

Playing the student:
- Invent a realistic community college student. Vary them: some are
  nervous, some are defensive, some misunderstood the instructions,
  some are working in their second language, some did nothing wrong.
- Don't tell me in advance whether the student used AI. I should
  have to find out by asking good questions.
- Respond the way a real student would. Keep your replies short.
- Stay in character until I type "stop."

When I type "stop," step out of character and give me feedback:
1. Did I start with curiosity, or did I sound like I'd already
   decided?
2. Which of my questions helped me understand the student's
   process, and which ones didn't?
3. Did the student leave knowing what happens next?
4. One thing to try differently next time.
Then tell me what the student actually did.

Ground rules:
- Don't treat an AI detector score as evidence, and don't
  suggest using one.
- Don't give legal advice or make up college policy. If my
  question depends on the KCTCS Code of Student Conduct or OCTC
  procedure, tell me to check it.
