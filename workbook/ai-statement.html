Three Levels of AI Use

A lot of course AI policies try to settle the question once for the whole course: students can use AI, or they can't. The trouble is that most courses have some assignments where AI could genuinely help and others where it would skip the learning. So instead of one rule, it works better to decide assignment by assignment. There are three levels to choose from.

No AI

Students complete the work on their own. This fits when the skill itself is what you're teaching, like a first draft where students work out their argument, math they need to be able to do without help, or a care plan they'll eventually write on the job. Choosing No AI for these assignments gives students the practice they need.

Example: An in-class reflection on a clinical experience, written in the student's own words.

AI-supported

Students can use AI for specific parts of the work, but the thinking and the final product are theirs. This fits when some parts of an assignment matter for learning and others don't. The key is to name which parts AI can help with. For example: "You can use AI to brainstorm and check grammar, but the analysis needs to be your own."

Example: A business plan where AI can help format the financial tables, but the market analysis and recommendations come from the student.

AI-integrated

Learning to use AI well is part of the assignment. This fits when students will run into AI in their field and need practice judging what it gives them. They might evaluate AI output, compare it to a standard, or explain how they used it.

Example: Welding students ask AI for a procedure, check it against the actual code, and mark what it got wrong.

Choosing a level

For each assignment, it helps to ask:

  1. What is this assignment meant to teach?
  2. Would AI help students learn that, or do the work for them?
  3. Will students use AI for this kind of work in their field?

If AI would do the learning for them, No AI is probably the right call. If it could handle the parts that aren't the focus, AI-supported may fit. If using AI well is something they'll need on the job, AI-integrated is worth considering.

A few tips

  • Tell students why you chose each level. They're more likely to follow an expectation they understand.
  • Put the level in the assignment instructions, not just the syllabus, since that's where students will look.
  • Most courses end up using more than one level, and that's normal.
  • You can always adjust a level next term if an assignment isn't working the way you hoped.

Paste this into Copilot. It will interview you one question at a time, then draft your statement, a disclosure sentence, and assignment labels. Don't paste in student names or student work, and read the draft carefully before you use it. Check anything that depends on OCTC or KCTCS policy.

I'm a community college instructor writing the AI use policy for my
course syllabus. My syllabus has a separate academic integrity section,
so this policy covers how students may and may not use AI in my course,
and why.

Interview me before you write anything. Ask one question at a time and
wait for my answer. Keep it to about 8 questions. If I'm unsure, give me
two or three options with the trade-offs. If my answers are vague or
contradict each other, gently push back. Don't draft the policy until
I say "ready."

Find out:
- My course and my students (program, level, online or in person)
- What students most need to learn to do on their own in this course
- My main assignments, and which level of AI use fits each one:
    No AI: students do the work without AI.
    AI-supported: AI can help with specific parts; students do the
    thinking and own the result.
    AI-integrated: using AI well is part of what the assignment teaches.
- Where I've seen AI help or get in the way of learning
- How students should tell me when and how they used AI
- What happens if a student uses AI in a way the policy doesn't allow,
  and how that connects to my academic integrity section

When I say "ready," write:
1. The policy, written to students in plain, warm, direct language,
   one page or less. Explain the reason behind each expectation,
   tell students what to do when they're unsure, and remind them not
   to put personal information or other people's work into AI tools.
2. A sample sentence students can use to disclose their AI use.
3. A one-line AI label for each assignment that I can paste into the
   assignment instructions.

Ground rules:
- Don't make up college policies, consequences, or approved tools.
  Flag anything that depends on OCTC or KCTCS policy for me to check.
- Make sure the policy doesn't conflict with disability accommodations
  or with tools multilingual students rely on. Flag anything I should
  check with accessibility services.
- Don't suggest AI detection tools as evidence.

After the draft, ask me what doesn't sound like me, and revise.
