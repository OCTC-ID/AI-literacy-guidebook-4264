Grading AI Judgment Through Evidence

In Exercise 2, you built one assignment around one behavior. One assignment shows you a single moment. The BE behaviors develop over a whole term, and the way to see that growth is to check in a few times, at points you plan ahead. Those are checkpoints.

A checkpoint doesn't need to be a new assignment. It's a few questions added to work students already turn in, like a short note at the end of a paper, three questions on a Blackboard submission, or a brief journal entry. What students write there is your evidence. Grading from that evidence is fairer and more consistent than grading from a general impression of how a student seems to be using AI.

Keep the weight low

The college framework treats the BE behaviors as something students develop, and they're graded only when a course writes them into a student learning outcome. Even then, keep the weight modest, around 5 to 10 percent of the course grade. Early in the term, your feedback does most of the work. The grade can come at the end, once students have had time to practice.

Protect honesty

These rules keep checkpoints from working against the behaviors they're meant to build:

  • Never take points off for disclosing AI use. If being honest costs points, students stop being honest. If a student used AI in a way your policy doesn't allow, handle that as a separate conversation, not through the checkpoint grade.
  • Grade reflections on how specific and honest they are. A student who writes clearly about a time AI made their work worse has done the reflection well. There's no "right" conclusion to reach.
  • Give students who don't use AI full credit for explaining their choice. Deciding not to use AI, and being able to say why, is good judgment.
  • Use a scenario for BE Safe. You usually can't see what a student put into a tool, so ask what they would share, check, or keep out of AI in a situation from your field.

What makes a plan work

  • Attach each checkpoint to an assignment students already do. In Blackboard, that can be a few questions on the submission or a short journal.
  • Focus each checkpoint on one or two behaviors. Five at once is too many to give useful feedback on.
  • Don't skip the Week 2 starting point. It's what lets the portfolio show growth, for students and for you.
  • Keep the questions the same from checkpoint to checkpoint where you can, so students can see their own answers change.

Three ways to grade it

Process points and a portfolio (recommended). Give completion points and feedback at each checkpoint during the term, then grade the final portfolio holistically. This keeps the stakes low while students are practicing and puts the grade where there's the most evidence.

An evidence-based rubric. If you want more structure, use one row per behavior, with the playbook stages as the levels. Rate each row from something the student turned in, like a disclosure note, a checking note, or a reflection. Here's a sample row:

Sample rubric row for BE Critical

BE CriticalUses AI output as givenChecks some claims, not consistentlyChecks against course or field standards and explains what they changed or rejected

Rows on a professionalism rubric. Technical and health programs often already grade professionalism. Adding one or two BE rows, usually Honest and Responsible, fits the behaviors into a rubric students already know.

I'm a community college instructor planning a few checkpoints across
one term to see how my students' judgment about AI develops. Each
checkpoint adds a few questions to an assignment I already give. I'm
not creating new assignments.

The five behaviors:
- BE Safe: knowing what goes into an AI tool and whether the tool
  fits the job.
- BE Honest: being accurate about what you did and what AI did.
- BE Critical: checking AI output before acting on it.
- BE Responsible: choosing on purpose whether AI fits the task, and
  owning the result.
- BE Reflective: noticing how using AI, or not using it, affects your
  learning, and adjusting.

Interview me before you write anything. Ask one question at a time
and wait for my answer. Keep it to about 6 questions. Find out:
- My course, my students, and the length of the term
- My main assignments and when they're due
- Which behaviors matter most in my course or field
- How I want to grade it: process points and a portfolio,
  a rubric, rows on a professionalism rubric, or feedback only

Don't draft until I say "ready." Then write:
1. A plan of 4 or 5 checkpoints: when, which assignment it attaches
   to, one or two behaviors, and what students turn in. Start with
   an ungraded starting point early in the term and end with a
   short portfolio or final reflection.
2. The 2 or 3 questions students answer at each checkpoint, written
   to students in plain, warm language. Keep them short enough to
   add to a Blackboard submission.
3. A suggestion for how much it counts and when it's graded.

Ground rules:
- Students never lose points for honestly disclosing AI use.
- Students who don't use AI can earn full credit by explaining
  their choice.
- Grade reflections on how specific and honest they are, not on
  reaching a particular conclusion.
- Assess BE Safe with a scenario from my field.
- Keep the total weight low, around 5 to 10 percent.
- Don't make up college policies. Flag anything that depends on
  OCTC or KCTCS policy for me to check.
- Don't suggest AI detection tools.

After the draft, ask me which checkpoint feels like the most work,
and help me make it lighter.
